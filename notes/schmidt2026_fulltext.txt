ABSTRACT
Context. Many proposed self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) models give rise to radiative processes that can dissipate energy. Un-
derstanding their impact on astrophysical objects through simulations and comparing the results with observations may thus constrain
SIDM models.
Aims. In this work, we systematically investigate how dissipation alters the gravothermal evolution of isolated SIDM halos by inde-
pendently varying dissipation and heat conduction and identify potential observational signatures.
Methods. To this end, we present the first extension of the N-body formalism for frequent small-angle self-interactions (fSIDM) to
include effective dissipation. We compare all results for isolated halos with a dissipative gravothermal fluid model to assess its validity
and limitations.
Results. We find that dissipation qualitatively changes the gravothermal evolution of SIDM halos beyond simply accelerating collapse.
Sufficiently strong central cooling can invert the usual role of heat conduction: the formation of an isothermal core is suppressed such
that conduction remains directed inward throughout the evolution. Outer halo regions beyond the scale radius can cool efficiently rather
than being heated by conduction, resulting in a larger region of mass infall and a less pronounced indentation between the core and the
outer halo in the final density profile. These effects depend strongly on the cooling rate but are comparatively insensitive to the angular
dependence of the self-interaction cross section. We further show that weakly dissipative self-interactions can explain the properties
of the recently observed strong lens perturber in JVAS B1938+666 with significantly shorter evolution times or, equivalently, smaller
cross sections compared to the elastic case.
Conclusions. Our results open a new route to connecting halo structure and recently reported compact objects to dark-sector micro-
physics.
Key words. dark matter – galaxies: halos – gravitation – scattering – methods: numerical – gravitational lensing: strong
1. Introduction
Self-interacting dark matter (SIDM) has emerged as a com-
pelling extension of the cold dark matter (CDM) paradigm
(Spergel & Steinhardt 2000; Tulin & Yu 2018; Adhikari et al.
2025), as it is well-motivated from a particle-physics perspec-
tive and addresses several small-scale challenges of the ΛCDM
model (see Bullock & Boylan-Kolchin 2017 for a review). Self-
interactions enable collisional heat conduction, which causes
a gravothermal evolution of dark matter (DM) halos with ini-
tial core formation and subsequent collapse (Balberg et al. 2002;
Koda & Shapiro 2011). The emerging time dependence of the
central density alleviates both the core-cusp problem and the too-
big-to-fail problem (Elbert et al. 2015).
SIDM models often invoke light mediators to achieve sizable
cross sections at dwarf-galaxy velocities (e.g., Tulin et al. 2013)
while satisfying constraints at cluster scales (e.g., Randall et al.
2008; Sagunski et al. 2021; Andrade et al. 2022). Recently, Yu
(2026) collected different indications of compact objects that
SIDM could explain. Most prominently, a dark million-solar-
mass object in the JVAS B1938+666 system at a redshift of z =
⋆E-mail: ludwig.schmidt@tum.de
0.881 has been identified via gravitational lensing (Powell et al.
2025; Vegetti et al. 2026). Its inferred mass distribution – an un-
resolved central point mass embedded in a nearly constant sur-
face density – appears to be consistent with a collapsing SIDM
halo. However, Vegetti et al. (2026) inferred that core collapse
by this redshift requires a velocity-averaged elastic cross section
as large as 800 cm2 g−1. This implies that the cross section would
have to increase very strongly toward low velocities.
As an alternative, dissipation is known to accelerate gravo-
thermal evolution (Essig et al. 2019), potentially allowing halos
to enter the collapse phase before z = 0.881 even for moderate
self-interaction cross sections. This mechanism has also been
proposed to explain the early formation of supermassive black
holes (Choquette et al. 2019; Xiao et al. 2021).
Light mediators (e.g., dark photons) naturally permit dissipa-
tive processes via bremsstrahlung (Lankester-Broche & Pradler
2026) if allowed kinematically. Dissipation can become particu-
larly efficient in the presence of DM bound states, which may be
collisionally excited and subsequently decay via mediator emis-
sion – closely analogous to radiative cooling in baryonic hydro-
gen during structure formation (Spitzer 1978). Models featur-
ing such bound states include mirror DM (Foot 2004), asym-

treats frequent small-angle scatterings effectively as a drag force
and diffusion acting on the simulation particles. In this work, we
present the first extension of this framework to include dissipa-
tion and apply it to isolated DM halos.
Using a simple velocity-independent parametrization, we
systematically investigate how dissipative self-interactions mod-
ify the gravothermal evolution of isolated DM halos by vary-
ing cooling and heat conduction independently. We further com-
pare the results with the gravothermal fluid model to assess
its validity and limitations, and with an isotropic implementa-
tion analogous to Shen et al. (2021) to examine the influence
of the cross section’s angular dependence. Finally, we derive
projected quantities accessible to gravitational lensing observa-
tions to identify potential observational signatures of dissipa-
tive SIDM. We further investigate whether dissipative SIDM can
generate the inferred mass profile of the strong lens perturber in
JVAS B1938+666 (Vegetti et al. 2026).
This work is structured as follows. In Sect. 2, we review the
fSIDM method and extend it to include dissipation. We present
our simulation results for isolated halos in Sect. 3, exploring
variations in dissipation and heat conduction. In Sect. 4, we in-
terpret these results and compute projected quantities to compare
them with lensing observations.

3. Simulation of an isolated, dissipative SIDM halo
Having validated our code (introduced in Sect. 2) in controlled
test setups without gravity (cf. Appendix B), we now apply it
to an isolated DM halo (described in Sect. 3.1). The goal is to
identify qualitative changes in the gravothermal evolution with
respect to the elastic SIDM case. We run two series of simu-
lations with increasing dissipation at constant heat conduction
(Sect. 3.2) and vice versa (Sect. 3.3) to disentangle their effects.
Finally, we compare the halo evolutions simulated with fSIDM,
i.e., with a forward-dominated cross section, with simulations
employing an isotropic rSIDM cross section (Sect. 3.4).
3.1. Set-up
Using SpherIC1 (Garrison-Kimmel et al. 2013), we generate
a discrete realization of a Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) halo
(Navarro et al. 1997) with scale radius rs = 3.6 kpc and charac-
teristic density ρ0 = 4ρ(rs) = 7.09 × 106 M⊙kpc−3, represented
by N = 5×106 particles. The code introduces an exponential cut-
off chosen beyond rcutoff = 55 kpc to render the total halo mass fi-
nite at a value of M = 1010 M⊙. We take this mass to equal M200c,
corresponding to a radius r200c ≃44 kpc, defined by an average
enclosed density of 200 times the critical density of the Universe
at z = 0. The virial radius thus lies well within the cutoff radius.
The concentration parameter c200c ≡r200c/rs ≃12 is somewhat
below the median mass-concentration relation, but is well within
the reported scatter (Dutton & Macciò 2014; Diemer & Joyce
2019).
Even at this comparatively high particle number, we find a
percent-level scatter in the initial energy of the sampled halo rel-
ative to the analytic expectation. This variation translates into
relative differences in the collapse time that are roughly ten times
larger (cf. Appendix C) because halos of lower initial energy be-
gin dissipation-driven collapse earlier. To mitigate this effect, we
generated multiple realizations and selected one with a relative
energy deviation of only 3 × 10−5.
Following Mace et al. (2024), we further verified that, for
this realization, the collapse time is insensitive to gravitational
softening lengths in the range ϵ = 4.0–63 pc. We adopt ϵ = 10 pc
for all simulations presented below.
We compare all N-body results with outputs from the
fluid model code GravothermalSIDM2 (Nishikawa et al. 2020;
Outmezguine et al. 2023; Gad-Nasr et al. 2024), augmented by
the cooling rate C(ρ, ν) from Eq. (19) as proposed by Essig et al.
(2019). Details are provided in Appendix D. In all subsequent
figures, the corresponding fluid-model results are shown in the
background for reference.

4. Discussion
In this section, we first provide a physical intuition for the simu-
lation results found, employing the fluid model to discuss the ra-
dially resolved halo energy budget (Sect. 4.1). Then, we draw the
connection to astrophysical observables by projecting along the
line of sight (Sect. 4.2) and discuss whether the projected den-
sity profile of the exotic compact object in JVAS B1938+666 in-
ferred by Vegetti et al. (2026) could be explained by dissipative
SIDM (Sect. 4.3).
4.1. Gravothermal evolution with dissipation
To interpret the results presented in Sect. 3, we adopt the sim-
plified picture of the gravothermal fluid model, which provides
a qualitatively accurate description as demonstrated. In this pic-
ture, heat conduction proceeds from regions of higher to lower
velocity dispersion (cf. Appendix D), i.e., both inward and out-
ward from the maximum of the initial NFW profile. As heat is
transported into the center, the central velocity dispersion in-
creases, causing particles to migrate to larger orbits. This leads to
an outward mass flow, reducing the central density and thereby
forming a core.
The structure of the core depends on the competition be-
tween conductive heating and dissipative cooling, the latter be-
ing most efficient at high densities. If conduction dominates, the
core is heated and expands, resulting in a lower central density.
Conversely, if cooling dominates, the core remains colder and
denser. Crucially, a fully isothermal core can only form in the
absence of dissipation.
In the presence of dissipation, we find that a more or less
pronounced off-center (r , 0) maximum in the velocity disper-
sion profile persists throughout the evolution (cf. Figs. 4 and 7,
lower rows). As a result, the direction of heat conduction in the
inner region does not reverse, in contrast to the elastic case. The
core loses energy locally through dissipation rather than con-
duction, without heating outer regions of the halo. Inward heat
conduction partially compensates for the energy loss, thereby de-
laying collapse. This explains why stronger conduction leads to
longer collapse times in our simulations (cf. Fig. 5), opposite to
the trend observed in the elastic case.
Using the fluid model, we can explicitly visualize this by de-
composing the rate of specific energy change into contributions
from conduction ˙qcond, dissipation ˙qdiss, and gravitational work
˙w as a function of radius and time (cf. Fig. 8). The dissipation
term ˙qdiss is non-positive at all radii and peaks in magnitude at
small radii where the density is highest. As expected, it vanishes
in the elastic case of rdiss = 1 (upper row).
In contrast, conduction ˙qcond only redistributes energy within
the halo and therefore integrates to zero over mass. Positive work
˙w > 0 is performed on regions that are compressed, while ex-
panding regions perform negative work ˙w < 0. By construction
of the fluid model, the sum of all contributions yields the change
in specific internal energy, which determines the velocity disper-
sion.
Initially, regions near the maximum of the velocity disper-
sion profile at r ∼rs = 3.6 kpc lose energy via conduction, trans-
ferring heat to both the core and the outer halo. The work term ˙w
reflects the gravitational response: heated regions generally ex-
pand, while cooling regions contract. These contributions do not
exactly balance, resulting in a net change in internal energy and
hence velocity dispersion.
In the elastic case (upper row), this process gradually erases
the maximum in the velocity dispersion profile. Once the max-

4.2. Consequences on lensing observables
To connect our simulation results to observables accessible to
strong-lensing surveys, we consider the time evolution of quan-
tities derived from the projected surface density Σ(r2D) (see Ap-
pendix F for definitions and calculation details). In particular,
Fig. 9 shows the evolutionary tracks of all simulations in the
plane spanned by the projected enclosed mass M(r < r2D) and
the logarithmic slope of the surface density profile γ2D(r2D)
evaluated at r = 0.2 kpc, r = 0.8 kpc, and r = 3.2 kpc (cf.
Fischer et al. 2025b). The arrows indicate increasing dissipation
(upper row) and heat conduction (lower row).
At r = 0.2 kpc, the core formation and collapse phase are
clearly distinguishable. Initially, the enclosed mass decreases
and the density profile becomes shallower until tcore (marked
by the circle), before both reverse. The overall variation of both
quantities throughout the evolution generally decreases as dis-
sipation dominates more strongly over heat conduction. This re-
flects the increasingly suppressed core formation. At r = 0.8 kpc,
the two phases remain visible for weak dissipation. For strong
dissipation, however, both the enclosed mass and profile steep-
ness increase monotonically from the beginning, indicating that
the core radius is smaller than 0.8 kpc. At r = 3.2 kpc, close to
the scale radius, the enclosed mass and profile steepness increase
immediately in all simulations, indicating that no core larger than
this radius forms.
Toward collapse, the non-dissipative simulation (blue, up-
per row) exhibits a clear turnaround in both quantities for r ≳
0.8 kpc. The earlier one in γ2D reflects the formation of an inden-
tation in the density profile caused by mass flowing both inward
and outward from the radius where ˙qcond = 0 (cf. Fig. 8, up-
per row). As this radius moves inward over time, outward mass
transport eventually dominates, leading to the second turnaround
in the enclosed mass.
Dissipation largely suppresses this outward mass transport,
at least at the radii considered here. As a result, the enclosed
mass at tcoll (squares) can exceed that of the elastic case and the
region of parameter space shaded in blue in Fig. 9 becomes ac-
cessible for moderate dissipation. For very strong dissipation,
however, the collapse time becomes so short that the enclosed
mass at tcoll is smaller, simply because the system has less time
to evolve. At fixed absolute time, the intuitive trend is restored:
stronger dissipation leads to larger enclosed mass, as particles
lose energy and fall inward. The turnaround in the slope persists,
but becomes progressively less pronounced with increasing dis-
sipation relative to conduction.
Once again, the gravothermal fluid model can reproduce
the physical trends described above. The precise values deviate
slightly.

4.3. Reproducing observed lensing results
We finally examine whether the gravothermal evolution of our
simulated halos can reproduce the mass distribution inferred by
Vegetti et al. (2026) for the compact object in JVAS B1938+666
detected through gravitational imaging (Powell et al. 2025).
Their analysis considered a broad class of density profiles, which
agree best in enclosed projected masses at the two radii r = 20 pc
and r = 90 pc. We therefore adopt the corresponding best-fit val-
ues M(r < 20 pc) = (4.25 ± 0.21) × 105 M⊙and M(r < 90 pc) =
(1.167 ± 0.039) × 106 M⊙. Taking these two data points as given,
we investigate whether our SIDM halos evolved with and with-
out dissipation could match them after appropriate rescaling
(cf. Appendix G). Recall that they are initially described by an
NFW profile and evolved in isolation. We further require an evo-
lution time consistent with the observed redshift zobs = 0.881.
In Fig. 10, we first consider the corresponding dimensionless
mass ratio M(r < r2D)/M(r < 4.5r2D) as a function of the inner
radius normalized by the NFW scale radius r2D/rs for all snap-
shots of the elastic and least dissipative simulation (rdiss = 1.01).
The gray band indicates the 3σ confidence region around the ob-
served mass ratio M(r < 20 pc)/M(r < 90 pc) = 0.364 ± 0.022
(dashed line). Close to core collapse, both the elastic and dissi-
pative SIDM halo match it over a wide range of scales.
Assuming that the density profile locally follows a simple
power law, we can map the shown mass ratio to the slope of the
projected density profile γ2D introduced in Sect. 4.2 (see sec-
ondary axis). Except for the shrinking core, the late-time den-
sity profile of the collapsing halo is much steeper than the inner
slope of the initial NFW profile and therefore in much better
agreement with the observation in both cases. For rdiss = 1.0,
the steep edge of the core causes an even larger mass ratio than
required for the observation. It shifts inward and amplifies with
time. The local minimum at r2D/rs ∼0.5 approaches the asymp-
totic slope γ2D = −1.19 of the self-similar fluid-model solution
in the LMFP limit (Balberg et al. 2002). For even larger radii,
the curves resemble the initial NFW profile as self-interactions
become inefficient. All this behavior generically falls within the
confidence interval of the observed mass ratio over about two
orders of magnitude in r/rs.
For rdiss = 1.01, the maximum in the mass ratio disappears
together with the steep section in the density profile (cf. Fig. 4).
The curves suggest the existence of a similar attractor slope as
in the elastic case, potentially even closer to the observed value.
Our simulations with rdiss > 1.01 do not reproduce the observa-
tion within the time accessible to the simulation. Whether they
would eventually do so if the simulations could be continued be-
yond numerical breakdown remains an open question and is a
key limitation of the present approach.
Having considered only scale-invariant quantities until now,
we explicitly rescale the simulated halo to the size of the ob-
served one in the following. We verified that Fig. 10 is fully in-
variant under the rescaling symmetry introduced in Appendix G,
as expected. However, the mass ratio may differ significantly for
initial conditions and cross sections that are not related by this
symmetry. We highlight three representative values of r2D/rs for
which the observed ratio can be reproduced within 3σ. In each
case, we compute the corresponding rescaling parameters as de-
scribed in Appendix G and list the rescaled physical halo prop-
erties in Table 2.
The larger the radius r2D/rs at which the halo shows the re-
quired mass ratio, the smaller the rescaled halo in extent. Since
the required mass rescaling varies much less, a smaller halo im-
plies a higher density ρ0, shorter evolution time tevo, and smaller
cross section σT/mχ. Multiple snapshots falling within the con-
fidence interval translate to a time interval ∆t, during which
the observation can be explained. This interval is significantly
shorter with dissipation. However, the values are only lower
bounds, as the halo likely remains within the confidence inter-
val beyond the simulation’s breakdown.

5. Conclusions
In this paper, we have introduced dissipation into the N-body
formalism for frequent small-angle self-interactions, described
by a drag force and transverse momentum diffusion. Compared
to the elastic case, the drag is increased by a generally velocity-
dependent dissipation parameter rdiss. The associated energy loss
is not fully restored by the transverse diffusion. Instead, it is cho-
Article number, page 11


===== PAGE 12 =====

A&A proofs: manuscript no. main
sen to reproduce the correct energy loss per collision of the un-
derlying model.
We applied the algorithm to an isolated DM halo with
different velocity-independent transfer cross sections σT/mχ
and dissipation parameters rdiss. At constant heat conduction
∝σT/mχrdiss, cooling ∝σT/mχ(rdiss −1) can accelerate gravo-
thermal evolution by orders of magnitude. Conversely, stronger
heat conduction slightly inhibits collapse – in contrast to the
elastic case, where it causes collapse.
The underlying reason is a qualitative difference in the evo-
lution of the velocity dispersion profile. If central dissipation is
sufficiently strong, the core does not become isothermal; instead,
the positive velocity dispersion gradient persists at the center. As
a consequence, conduction remains directed inward and counter-
acts central dissipation, which ultimately drives the collapse. In
outer halo regions, where dissipation also dominates over out-
ward conduction, we observe mass infall rather than outflow. In
some cases, this leads to more enclosed mass at large radii than
is achievable without dissipation and may generally increase the
mass of a potentially forming central object.
The aforementioned may be the most distinct signatures
of dissipation. For moderate dissipation, however, the observ-
ables evolve in general very similarly, only faster. Using rescal-
ing arguments, we could explicitly show that the observed
mass distribution of the recently discovered compact object in
JVAS B1938+666 can be explained by both an elastic and a
weakly dissipative SIDM halo. In the late stages of their evolu-
tion, they generically develop steep slopes in the density profile,
consistent with the observation. With dissipation, the required
evolution time can be reduced by roughly a factor of two, or
equivalently, a smaller cross section suffices.
Dissipation can therefore provide a natural mechanism to
evade constraints on the self-interaction cross section. Realis-
tically, both the cross section σT/mχ and rdiss depend on ve-
locity and are explicitly connected to a particle physics model.
An emerging angular dependence may require a dissipative gen-
eralization of the hybrid approach between fSIDM and rSIDM
(Arido et al. 2025). To generalize the conclusions of this work,
initial conditions outside the rescaling family considered here
should be explored, along with methods to continue simulations
deeper into the collapse phase. Finally, cosmological simulations
of dissipative SIDM will be essential to derive observational con-
straints on the dissipation. Taken together, our results motivate a
broader theoretical and observational program to constrain the
dissipative properties of DM and their implications for particle
physics models of the dark sector.
